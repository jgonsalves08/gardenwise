Garden plans that fit your space, skill, budget and frost dates — for first-time gardeners, households, schools and community gardens.

In one sentence: Gardenwise turns a one-minute form — or one sentence typed to its garden helper — into a garden plan (plants, bed layout, a dated calendar built from local frost data, and a costed shopping list) whose AI picks are checked by code, and then helps you track the season, from a single balcony up to a 40-plot community garden.

2. Motivation

This project started with my own problem. I want to start a garden, but I didn't know what to plant, when to plant it, how much space or money it would take, or where to begin. Every answer seemed to depend on something I didn't know yet.

I'm not alone. The National Gardening Association counted 18.3 million new gardeners in 2020 alone, and food-gardening households rose from 33% to 42% of US households that year. New gardeners have a rough first season: in a HomeAdvisor survey of more than 1,000 US gardeners, people who had been gardening for under a month reported losing about half of their plants, roughly double the loss rate of experienced gardeners. The usual causes are planting at the wrong time, choosing plants that don't suit the spot, and under- or over-watering.

There is also a safety problem that beginners rarely know about. Older yards, city lots and roadsides often have lead in the soil: a CUNY study of 1,652 samples from 904 New York City gardens found 71% of home-garden samples exceeded New York State's soil limits for lead and arsenic, and a survey of 130+ Boston backyard gardens found more than 85% above the EPA's reportable level. Pets are a second hidden risk: tomato plants, garlic, onions, chives, mint and lavender are on the ASPCA's toxic list for cats and dogs.

How people deal with it today

  • Piecing it together: seed packets, a state extension office's planting calendar PDF, YouTube, Reddit and garden-center advice. Each answers one question, none know your space, budget or frost dates, and they often disagree.
  • Asking ChatGPT: fast, but it invents details, doesn't know your exact frost dates or budget, can't be checked, and forgets your garden once the chat ends (Section 3 measures this).
  • Garden-planner apps: good at drawing beds and calendars, but they assume you already know what to grow, and almost all of them stop at one person's garden.

What it costs them: time (an evening or more of research for a first plan, then guesswork all season), money (a 4×8 raised bed with bagged soil can run $250–$350 before a single seed, and beginners often overbuy), and frustration when half the plants die and they quit. Schools and community gardens multiply this: a coordinator has to plan dozens of plots for people of different skill levels, keep everyone on schedule, and split costs fairly, usually in spreadsheets and group emails.

Why it matters: a successful first season turns into fresh food, lower grocery bills, outdoor time with kids, and gardeners who come back next year. Gardens at schools and community lots reach people who can't garden at home.

Sources: National Gardening Association, 2021 National Gardening Survey (via garden.org and AP coverage); HomeAdvisor gardener survey (reported by Gardening Etc); Cheng et al., CUNY/Soil Science (2015) via The FERN; Boston backyard garden XRF screening, GSA 2006 Annual Meeting abstract; ASPCA Toxic and Non-Toxic Plants list.

What Gardenwise does differently, and why it matters

  1. It starts from the beginner's question ("what should I even grow?") instead of assuming you already know. Goals like salsa, pesto or a kids' garden, plus skill level, drive the plant picks.
  2. AI suggestions are checked, not trusted. The AI may only pick plant IDs from a 48-plant database, and code verifies zone, sun, skill, space, frost-based dates, school-year timing, crop rotation, pets and lead before you see anything. Against a plain prompt to the same model, plants passing every check rose from 72% to 100% (Section 7).
  3. It respects a budget. Every quantity and price is computed in code (lumber, soil in bags vs bulk, pots, seeds), with cheaper alternatives when you're over budget.
  4. It scales from one person to a whole community garden, which no tool I found does: household sizing, school-year calendars with classroom tie-ins, and community gardens with join codes, organizer/admin/member roles, CSV import of a sign-up sheet (every plot planned in one batch), shared work days, pooled seed orders and a cost split.
  5. It stays useful all season: weekly to-dos, editable task dates, one-tap harvest logging, a dashboard, and "plan next season here" with crop-rotation advice.

A garden planner can look like the "trip or meal planner" ideas the course warns about. The difference is that Gardenwise isn't a one-time itinerary: it's a season-long tool whose AI output is verified against real data, and its community-garden mode serves groups, not just individuals.

4. The agents

Gardenwise uses five AI agents and three code "agents." The rule I followed: the AI chooses; code computes and checks. Anything with a right answer (dates, quantities, prices, safety rules) is code.

How they are organized

The agents form a code-orchestrated pipeline rather than a "boss" agent delegating to specialists. Each step has a fixed job and a strict input and output, so runs are predictable, cheap and easy to test. The free-form agent is the garden helper, which uses tools to reach the same code.

Model choice

All AI agents use gpt-6-luna through the course's Portkey gateway: it's the model the course key allows, and it's inexpensive ($0.10 per million input tokens, $0.01 cached, $0.50 output). It supports everything I needed: structured JSON output, function calling (the helper) and image input (the Site Scout). I run it at low reasoning effort, because each task is narrow and the code verifier catches mistakes. If a picks list fails, the verifier sends it back once instead of paying for heavier reasoning on every call. A 40-plan A/B test against medium effort found no quality difference (Section 5).

Scaling up: schools and community gardens

The same pipeline runs per plot. A school garden gets harvests timed to the school year (a summer-care rota appears only if someone can tend it in July) and a classroom tie-in for every crop. A community-garden organizer uploads a member sign-up CSV; every row is planned in one capped batch; members join with a code and claim plots; the garden gets shared work days, a pooled seed order and a cost split, with garden-wide results visible to everyone and per-plot detail only to admins.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Code does everything with a right answer: dates, layout, quantities, prices, verification, exports, dashboards. Ticking or unticking plants re-plans instantly with zero AI calls.
  2. Prompt caching: the long, unchanging part of each prompt (rules plus the 48-plant catalog) comes first, and the short gardener profile comes last, with a prompt_cache_key per agent. Warm plans reuse about {{CACHE_PCT}} of their input tokens at one-tenth the price.
  3. Small outputs: the Selector returns plant IDs and one-line reasons, not a written plan; alternates come back in the same call, so a user can switch plants without a new call.
  4. Low reasoning effort plus a verifier with one retry, instead of an expensive model on every call.
  5. Low-detail images for the Site Scout (about 1,000 input tokens for a photo check) and no image storage.
  6. Hard caps: 60,000 tokens and 25 AI calls per browser session, ≤ 2 intake follow-ups, ≤ 4 tool steps per helper message, and a sized cap for each community CSV batch.

Did cost cutting hurt performance?

I tested both cost cuts directly. Caching: cold and warm runs produced the same quality (100% of plans passed the verifier on the first try in both modes), while caching cut the cost per plan by 37%. Low reasoning effort: scripts/effort_ab.py planned the same 10 profiles twice each at low and at medium effort (40 plans, order alternated so neither setting got a warmer cache) and scored them with the same code checks:

{{table:effort}}

Medium effort cost about 25% more per plan and was several times slower, with no measurable gain: every plan passed at both settings, and both settings chose largely the same plants (77% overlap). That supports running at low effort and letting the code verifier catch mistakes. A plain one-prompt plan from the same model is slightly cheaper still (it skips the build advisor) but much less reliable (Section 7). Caveat: 20 plans per setting is a small sample, and these checks measure validity, not taste; a higher setting might write better reasons or pick more interesting plants, which these checks don't score.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Hot climates: Florida, the Gulf Coast, South Texas, the low deserts and coastal Southern California have limited support. Their season is set by summer heat, not frost.
  • Data depth: 48 plants and 111 reference cities. Frost dates are 50/50 averages for the nearest reference city, a few mountain towns' values are rough, and yields and prices are estimates (prices as of fall 2025, with store links to check).
  • Not yet tested with real organizers or schools. The community and school modes are verified with test accounts and automated tests, not with a real coordinator or teacher.
  • Local deployment: runs from a clone with a local SQLite database. A public launch needs hosting, a managed database and account recovery (there's no email by design).
  • AI variability: picks can vary between runs. The verifier keeps them safe and valid, but not identical.
  • Photos are a snapshot: the Site Scout can't measure sun hours or dimensions, so it reports confidence and asks the user to confirm.

What I'd build with another month

  1. Pilot with a real community garden and a school (for example, a New Haven community garden and an elementary school), measuring planning time, cost per plot and the share of plots that reach harvest.
  2. Hot-climate calendars based on heat and day length, so the South and Southwest get full support.
  3. More plants and varieties, plus perennials and fruit trees, and live weather (frost alerts) in a hosted version.
  4. Problem diagnosis: a photo of a yellowing leaf → likely causes from extension guides, verified like the plant picks.
  5. Hosting with a managed database, email reminders (opt-in) and data export, so organizations can rely on it.
